# Module 1: Show me the page, not the vibe

## Lesson: Show me the page, not the vibe

Show me the page, not the vibe. This course runs on that rule, and the rule came out of a near miss in our own marketing.
In July 2026 a CARSI brand post was drafted with help from an AI writing tool. Its headline made a confident claim about what an industry standard did not contain. It read well and it sounded certain.
The claim came from a web scrape: an unofficial copy of a document, searched for a word, with nothing found. That kind of absence claim can't be proven from a web copy, and it could not be backed by CARSI's licensed copy, so it never went out. CARSI's pre-publish checks caught it, and the checks were tightened so the same kind of claim is blocked automatically next time.
Nobody set out to mislead anyone. The draft was confident, and AI tools are good at sounding confident. The fix was not a better prompt. The fix was asking for the page.

### Learning objectives
By the end of this course you will be able to:

- check a claim about a regulator, a study or a standard against the page it comes from;
- state Australia's ozone exposure limit and when the new Workplace Exposure Limits start;
- explain what the evidence does and does not show about ozone and hydroxyl generators;
- set up controls and job records if an ozone treatment goes ahead.

### Why this matters on a job
You get the same confident claims on site. A sales rep says a machine kills mould. A forum post says hydroxyl is safe with the family at home. A client has read that ozone fixes smoke smell overnight. Each of those may be partly true, wholly true or false. You can't tell from how sure the person sounds.
So before you repeat a claim to a client, an insurer or your own crew, ask who is saying it, whether they are the body responsible for it, and whether you can open the page they are relying on. If you can't open it, treat it as a lead to check, not a fact.

### How sources are ranked in this course

- Australian regulators (Safe Work Australia, WorkSafe Victoria, the TGA) for what the law and the exposure limits are.
- Peer-reviewed research and public-health agencies (for example the US EPA) for what the science shows.
- Manufacturer spec sheets for what a machine is on paper: power draw, output, settings. Never as proof that it is safe or that it works.
- Trade press, sales material, social posts and AI chat answers are leads. Check them against one of the above before you rely on them.
Every source in this course carries the date we read it. Regulator pages change. The US EPA page on ozone generators used in the next lesson showed a last-updated date of 19 February 2026 when we read it. If you are reading this a year from now, open the page again.

### How CARSI handles industry standards
CARSI's policy: we do not reproduce the text of industry standards in our courses, and we do not paste it into AI tools. This course makes no claim about what any industry standard says about ozone or hydroxyl generators.
If a standard matters on your job, read your own licensed copy, and treat anyone else's summary of it, including an AI tool's, as a lead to check.

### A common mistake
Repeating a claim because it came from someone senior, or because an AI tool said it with a reference attached. A reference you have not opened is not a check. The July post had the look of a fact-check. It just had nothing underneath it.

# Module 2: Ozone and the evidence

## Lesson: Ozone: the Australian exposure limit and what the evidence shows

Ozone is a reactive gas. An ozone generator makes it on purpose. The California Air Resources Board describes some devices advertised as air purifiers as purposely emitting large amounts of ozone.

### The Australian exposure limit
In Australian workplaces, ozone has a peak limitation of 0.1 ppm (0.2 mg/m&sup3;). A peak limitation is the maximum concentration a worker can be exposed to, measured over the shortest time possible and not exceeding 15 minutes, and exposure above it is not allowed at any time. WorkSafe Victoria states the same figure for ozone.
Safe Work Australia's Workplace Exposure Standards (WES) apply until 30 November 2026. From 1 December 2026 they are replaced by the Workplace Exposure Limits (WEL). The July 2025 WEL list keeps ozone at a peak limit of 0.1 ppm (0.2 mg/m&sup3;), so the number does not change at the switchover. Check your state or territory regulator for how it applies where you work.
An exposure limit is a limit for workers, not a level that is safe for everyone. Safe Work Australia describes exposure limits as the maximum level that most (but not all) people can be exposed to without harm to their health, and says the risk must still be eliminated or kept as low as reasonably practicable whether or not there is a limit.
WorkSafe Victoria lists the health effects of ozone exposure as irritation of the eyes, nose, throat and lower airways, reduced lung function, worse asthma and chronic respiratory disease, greater susceptibility to respiratory infection, and lung damage that may continue after symptoms have gone. The US EPA adds that relatively low amounts of ozone can cause chest pain, coughing, shortness of breath and throat irritation, and that people vary widely in their susceptibility to ozone. This course does not give medical advice. If someone feels unwell after exposure, follow your first-aid procedure and get medical help.

### Does it work?
The US EPA's summary of the research says that at concentrations that do not exceed public health standards, ozone does not effectively remove viruses, bacteria, mould or other biological pollutants. It says some data suggest that low levels of ozone may reduce some airborne organisms while the ozone is present, but that concentrations would have to be 5 to 10 times higher than public health standards allow before ozone could decontaminate the air well enough to stop the organisms surviving once it is removed. It also says that even at high concentrations ozone may have no effect on contaminants embedded in porous material such as duct lining or ceiling tiles.
On odour, the EPA says there is evidence that, at concentrations that do not exceed public health standards, ozone is not effective at removing many odour-causing chemicals, and that there are few scientific studies supporting the claim that ozone removes odours. It says it is plausible that some odour chemicals react with ozone: in some experiments ozone appeared to react readily with some chemicals behind new-carpet smell, and ozone is believed to react with acrolein, one of the odorous chemicals in second-hand tobacco smoke. In an experiment built to mimic formaldehyde levels in an embalming studio, ozone had no effect on formaldehyde, and other experiments suggest body odour may be masked by the smell of ozone but not removed by it.
Ozone also makes new chemicals. In a laboratory experiment with new-carpet chemicals, the EPA reports that ozone produced a range of aldehydes and the total organic chemicals in the air went up, not down. Ozone may also raise indoor formic acid.

### High-concentration treatments in empty buildings
The EPA notes that high ozone concentrations are sometimes used in unoccupied spaces, and gives fire restoration as an example. It also says little is known about the by-products these treatments leave behind, that conditions should be controlled well enough that no person or pet is exposed, and that ozone can damage plants, rubber, electrical wire coatings, and fabrics and artwork with susceptible dyes.
A controlled study has shown ozone can inactivate some microbes under tightly controlled conditions. In a 2021 study from a German university hospital, an automated ozone system built to raise room humidity as well as make ozone was run in a gas-tight test room. It cut two surrogate viruses on tile, stainless steel and furniture board by more than 4 log10. The authors said more work was needed on safe use in practice. A sealed test room at high humidity is a long way from a wet house with gaps under the doors.
The EPA also reports that results of some controlled studies show ozone concentrations considerably higher than health standards are possible even when the user follows the manufacturer's instructions. CARB recommends ozone generators not be used except for approved industrial purposes where harmful exposure is prevented, and strongly advises against them in spaces occupied by people or animals.

### A judgement call
A client asks you to "just run the ozone machine" for a musty smell. A musty smell can point to a source: wet subfloor, a mouldy carpet underlay, a leaking pipe in a wall. The EPA ranks source control first, ventilation second and air cleaning third. Find and fix the source. A machine that masks the smell can hide the problem you were called in to find.

# Module 3: Hydroxyl generators and the evidence

## Lesson: Hydroxyl generators: what is known and what is not

The hydroxyl radical (OH) is the main oxidant in outdoor air. Indoors its role is secondary to ozone. A 2026 review by Weschler and Nazaroff found that the primary sources of OH in occupied buildings are reactions between ozone and compounds such as limonene.
Hydroxyl generators are machines built to make OH on purpose. If you have been offered one for an odour job, the question is what published research has measured.

### What the research has measured
A 2021 Georgia Institute of Technology study ran a commercial hydroxyl radical generator in an office and measured what came out. While it ran, oxygenated volatile organic compounds rose, especially low-molecular-weight organic acids. Particle numbers and mass rose quickly as highly oxidised secondary organic aerosol formed. The authors said future studies should look past whether a device kills bioaerosol or cuts one chemical, and should also measure the by-products it creates.
A 2021 perspective by Collins and Farmer in Environmental Science & Technology looked at air cleaners that work by chemistry. It argues that adding reactive chemicals or processes indoors can release reactive intermediates and by-products, that air cleaners often run continuously, and that exposure to what they produce may be a significant risk. It called for more study of chemically reactive air cleaning before broader adoption.

### What this course could not establish
The sources used for this course do not show how well hydroxyl generators remove odour on Australian restoration jobs, or whether a given unit is safe to run with people inside. That is a gap in what we could verify, not a finding that the machines fail. Treat any claim about odour removal or occupied use as a claim to be checked.

### Ask for the test report
When a supplier says a hydroxyl unit removes smoke odour or is safe with the family at home, ask for the test report behind it. Look for:

- who did the testing, and whether they were independent of the seller;
- the room size, the odour source and how the result was measured;
- the measured ozone output of the unit, in ppm, at the setting you would use;
- whether by-products such as particles and organic acids were measured at all.
If the answer is a brochure, you have a claim, not evidence. Write down what you were told and by whom.

### A common mistake
Assuming a machine is safe to run with people inside because the box says it is not an ozone generator. The Georgia Tech study measured new particles and organic acids from a hydroxyl unit, which is why CARSI recommends keeping people out until you know what yours produces.

# Module 4: On the job in Australia

## Lesson: On an Australian job: claims, controls and records

### What the TGA does and does not assess
TGA guidance from August 2021 lists "air sanitisers and deodorisers (e.g. ozone)" among its examples of sanitation and environmental control equipment, which the guidance treats as excluded goods that the TGA does not regulate, and it says goods within Schedule 1 of the Therapeutic Goods (Excluded Goods) Determination 2018 are excluded irrespective of how they are advertised or presented for supply. In the guidance's own example, an air sanitiser is not considered sanitation or environmental control equipment because it is designed to have an effect in the human body, so it is a medical device that must be included in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods before it is imported or supplied. So a machine being on sale in Australia does not tell you whether the TGA has assessed it.
The same guidance says excluded products remain subject to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010, administered by the ACCC, and that advertising must not mislead or deceive. That covers what you write in a quote, on a van or on your website. CARSI's advice: if you say a treatment kills viruses or removes mould, be ready to show the evidence behind it.
One point catches restorers who fog: the TGA says liquid disinfectants with specific claims, dispensed through automated airborne or fogging systems, are not excluded goods and must be included in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods.

### Controls if you do run an ozone generator
WorkSafe Victoria recommends ozone generators with alarms and automatic shut-off, a system to monitor and alert for high ozone levels, maintenance as the manufacturer recommends, and training for everyone who uses the machines, including what to do if ozone is detected. On a restoration job, CARSI's recommended practice adds:

- Clear the space of people and pets before you start, and keep it clear. Sign every entry point and lock it if you can.
- Remove or protect plants, rubber items, artwork and susceptible fabrics, which the EPA says ozone can damage.
- Use a calibrated ozone meter and measure rather than assume. Your crew must not be exposed above the 0.1 ppm peak limit, and that limit is for workers. It is not a clearance level for anyone else.
- Check the rating plate matches Australian mains before you plug in. Australian supply is nominally 230 V, according to the Australian Energy Regulator. Run it from a GPO protected by an RCD, and keep it in your company's test-and-tag program.

### Re-entry is a site-specific decision
The 0.1 ppm peak limit is a workplace exposure limit. Safe Work Australia says exposure limits protect most but not all people from harm, and that the risk must still be eliminated or kept as low as reasonably practicable regardless of the limit. So a reading below it is not, on its own, clearance for the family, a tenant or anyone else to go back in.
When occupants can return is a site-specific decision. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the unit and any guidance from your state or territory regulator that applies, and measure rather than assume: base the decision on calibrated readings, not on the smell fading or the time on the clock. Two cautions from the US EPA apply. Ozone concentrations considerably higher than health standards are possible even when the manufacturer's instructions are followed. And little is known about the by-products that high-concentration treatments leave behind, so a low ozone reading does not tell you those have gone. WorkSafe Victoria also reminds employers that they must, so far as is reasonably practicable, make sure people other than their employees are not exposed to health and safety risks from the work.
CARSI's recommendation: if you cannot show why the space is ready for the people going back in, treat it as not ready.

### Write it down
Your job record should let someone else see exactly what you did. Record the make, model and serial number of the unit, the setting, the start and stop times, who isolated the area, the meter used and its calibration date, the readings you took and when, who decided occupants could go back in and on what basis, and what you removed or protected. Record any claim you made to the client about what the treatment would do, and what you based it on.

### A judgement call
The insurer wants the smoke smell gone by Friday and the family is staying in the house. That is not an ozone job. Source removal, cleaning and ventilation come first. If a device treatment is still needed, schedule it for when the house can be emptied, and decide when people go back in from the manufacturer's instructions, any applicable guidance and your measured readings.

### Key takeaways

- Australia's workplace exposure limit for ozone is a peak of 0.1 ppm (0.2 mg/m&sup3;), not to be exceeded at any time, and the figure carries over to the WEL from 1 December 2026. It is a limit for workers, not clearance for occupants to return.
- At concentrations that do not exceed public health standards, the EPA says ozone does not effectively remove biological pollutants, and it found few studies supporting odour removal. Those standards are not a promise of safety: the EPA says relatively low amounts of ozone can harm people, and susceptibility varies widely.
- A 2021 study of a commercial hydroxyl generator measured by-products forming while it ran. Proof of odour removal on real jobs was not found in the sources for this course.
- TGA guidance lists air sanitisers and deodorisers (e.g. ozone) among its examples of excluded sanitation and environmental control equipment, which the TGA does not regulate, but an air sanitiser designed to have an effect in the human body is not that equipment: the guidance treats it as a medical device. Australian consumer law still applies to what you claim about them.
- Ask for the page. Write down what you did.
