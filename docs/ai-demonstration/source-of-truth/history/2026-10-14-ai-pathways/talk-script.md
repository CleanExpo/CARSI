## 1:00 to 3:30 | Why now: three shifts (2:30)

[Slide 2.]

Three things changed this year. I'll keep each one short.

First: the agents moved into the desktop app. An agent is just an AI that does a job in several steps instead of answering one question. A year ago you needed a terminal and some nerve to run one. Now it's a button.

Anthropic's Cowork went generally available on Mac and Windows on 9 April. You describe the outcome you want. It works through your files and your browser. Microsoft has Copilot Tasks, which takes a goal in plain language, browses sites, edits files and uses your connected email and storage. Google has Gemini Tasks, but that one needs a paid Google AI Pro or Ultra plan and a personal Google account.

A warning. Microsoft's own page says Tasks is moving into something called Copilot Cowork. The names change monthly. Don't memorise names. Learn the pattern.

[Slide 3.]

Second shift: scheduled work. Claude added scheduled, recurring tasks on 25 February. Anthropic says those run in the cloud, so your computer doesn't need to be awake. Though if a task touches files on your machine, the app has to be open. Gemini is stricter: its schedules don't run if your device is off. Read the fine print for whatever you choose.

Think about what that means for a small crew of four or five. The Monday paperwork that used to eat your morning can be ready before you've had your coffee. You still check it. But you're checking, not typing.

So "while I sleep" is now a setting. For years the ceiling was: type a question, read the answer, copy it somewhere. That ceiling is gone.

[Slide 4.]

Third shift: the AI can reach your stuff, and remember you. In July, Claude's Microsoft 365 connector gained write tools, and your admin has to approve it. In August, memory started working across chat and Cowork, with a setting that keeps sensitive topics out. And on 17 August MYOB announced that customer balances, overdue invoices and profit and loss would show up inside Claude and ChatGPT. That's a phased rollout, so check whether you've got it yet.

Memory deserves a plain explanation. It means the app keeps notes about you and your business between chats, so you stop repeating yourself. You can see those notes and edit them. That's useful, and it's also exactly why the privacy settings matter. More on that soon.

Agents, schedules, connections. Hold onto those. They are the three shifts, and the ladder is how you climb them.

## 3:30 to 7:00 | The ladder, L0 to L5 (3:30)

[Slide 5.]

The ladder has six rungs, numbered zero to five. You don't skip rungs. Each one makes the next safer, because by the time an agent is touching your invoices you've already learned how it goes wrong on something small. I'll give you one small-business example for each, and one trap.

[Slide 6.]

Rung zero: your first conversation. Take a customer email, rub out the customer's name and contact details first, and paste it in. Ask for a polite reply in your voice. Then ask, "What did I miss?" That second question is the useful one. The trap: believing the answer is a fact. It's a draft. You're still the one signing it.

Rung one: better prompts and documents. Upload last month's price list and a job note. Ask for a quote draft as a table. Then ask the AI to question your assumptions. The trap is what I call prompt tourism. You collect new tricks every day and never get a result you can repeat. You're ready to move up when you've reused the same instruction twice and it saved you time.

Rung two: an AI that knows your business. Here you build a Project, a standing workspace holding your services, your pricing rules, your tone and three sample jobs. Claude's free plan gives you up to five Projects, and paid plans are unlimited. You're ready when a new question about your business gets the right answer without you explaining everything again. The trap is stale or contradictory files. If your price list from 2023 is in there, the AI will use it. That's the demo in a minute.

[HUMAN INPUT NEEDED: Phill story about the Project he relies on most in his own business]

[Slide 7.]

Rung three: connected. The AI reads your Gmail or Microsoft 365, your calendar, your Drive. Try this: "List customer emails I haven't answered in two days. Draft replies. Don't send." Read-only first. The trap is permissions that are too broad, and trusting a number without checking it. Pick five answers and check each against the source before you believe the sixth.

Rung four: delegating. Give an agent a folder of ten job notes and ask for one weekly summary spreadsheet. Cowork has three approval modes: Manual, Auto and Skip. Stay on Manual. The trap is using Skip anywhere near money or customer messages. Vague goals hurt too, and so does burning through your usage limit on a job that was never clear.

Rung five: work while you sleep. Monday, 7am: summarise last week's enquiries and overdue invoices, and draft the chasers. Draft. Not send. The trap is an unattended task that emails customers, with nobody checking its work.

Notice how the examples are all boring. Replies, quotes, summaries, chasers. That's deliberate. The wins in a small business come from boring jobs done every week without fuss.

And the real test of rung five isn't the schedule. It's whether you've written down who owns this, what's allowed, and who signs off. One page. We'll get to that.

## 10:00 to 12:00 | Safety and privacy (2:00)

[Slide 9.]

Here's what the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner says. The Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles apply to personal information you put into an AI tool, and to AI output that contains personal information. If the AI makes up personal information about someone, that counts too. The OAIC also tells businesses to do their homework on any product before they use it, and to update their privacy policy to match. And it you don't enter personal information, sensitive information especially, into publicly available AI tools. Think about what's in your job files: names, addresses, photos of the inside of someone's home, sometimes a health detail on a claim. That's the stuff to keep out.

[HUMAN INPUT NEEDED: Phill story about a privacy mistake or near miss with AI]

I'm not a lawyer, and I'm not telling you whether the Act covers your business. That's worth a call to someone who is. But the habit is cheap: leave names and contact details out.

[Slide 10.]

Second thing: each tool has different rules about training. For Claude's personal plans, Anthropic says your chats are used to improve models if you opt in, or if they're flagged for safety. For Gemini, chats can train models when Keep Activity is on. Turn it off, or use Temporary Chat, and Google says they aren't used for training and are kept for 72 hours. Different tools. Different switches. Check each one you use.

[Slide 11.]

Third: the one-page policy. The National AI Centre's Guidance for AI Adoption sets out six practices. There's a Foundations version written for people just starting out. Here's my plain reading of the six, as lines on your page.

Accountability: one named person owns AI in this business. Impact planning: what could go wrong for a customer. Risk management: what we never paste in. Information sharing: we're open with staff and customers about where AI is involved. Testing and monitoring: someone checks the outputs every month. Human control: nothing reaches a customer until a person has read it.

One page. Six lines. Put a name next to the first one.

## 12:00 to 13:30 | Find your rung (1:30)

[Slide 12. Handout in hand.]

Time for you to work. Page one of the handout has eight questions. Each answer is worth zero to three points. Score yourself honestly, nobody's marking it. You've got sixty seconds.

[Wait 60 seconds. Ask for quiet.]

Add it up. Zero to three, you're at rung zero. Four to eight, rung one. Nine to twelve, rung two. Thirteen to sixteen, rung three. Seventeen to twenty, rung four. Twenty-one and up, rung five.

One catch, and it's for the people who've been playing with AI for a year or more. Look at questions three, four and five. If all three are an A or a B, you're still at the chatbot stage, whatever your total says. Start at rung two and rung three. Usage isn't the same as depth.

Hands up if you came out lower than you expected. Yeah. That's fine. It's the point of the quiz. A low score today just tells you where the first useful job is.

## 13:30 to 15:00 | Close: one action this week (1:30)

[Slide 13.]

Two groups, one job each.

If you're a beginner, scoring zero to eight: do rung zero this week. Take one real customer email. Remove the name and contact details. Paste it into any free app and ask for a reply, then ask what you missed. Fifteen minutes. Then write down one thing you won't ever paste in.

If you've used AI for a year or more: build one Project this week. Your services, your pricing rules, your tone and three sample jobs, all stripped of customer details. Test it with a new enquiry, and fix the files when it gets something wrong. Then run the question I asked in the demo: what assumptions did you make?
